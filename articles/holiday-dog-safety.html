Quick answer

The biggest holiday dangers for dogs are chocolate, raisins and grapes (in mince pies, Christmas cake and stollen), xylitol, onions and garlic (in stuffing and gravy), cooked bones and fatty leftovers, plus tinsel, ribbon, salt dough ornaments, chewed light cables and fireworks. Keep food and decorations out of reach, give your dog a quiet room, and if they eat something risky, call your vet straight away, even if they seem fine.

The holidays are the busiest time of year in most vet clinics. Not because owners are careless, but because the house suddenly fills with new smells, new food, new objects at nose height and a steady stream of visitors.

This guide covers every common holiday hazard, from Thanksgiving through Christmas to New Year's Eve fireworks: where each danger hides, what signs to watch for, and exactly what to do. There's a room-by-room checklist you can work through in 15 minutes and an emergency plan to read before you need it.

Toxic holiday foods and where they hide

Most holiday poisonings happen because a dangerous ingredient is hidden inside something that looks harmless. Use this table to spot them.

DangerWhere it hides at the holidaysSigns to watch for
Chocolate (dark and baking chocolate are the most dangerous)Selection boxes, tree decorations, advent calendars, chocolate logs, brownies, presents under the treeVomiting, diarrhoea, restlessness, panting, a racing heart, tremors. Signs often start within 2 to 12 hours.
Grapes, raisins, sultanas and currantsMince pies, Christmas cake, Christmas pudding, stollen, panettone, fruit loaf, cheese boardsVomiting, lethargy, not eating. Can cause kidney damage, and there's no known safe amount.
Xylitol (also listed as birch sugar)Sugar-free gum and mints in guests' bags, sugar-free sweets and baking, some peanut buttersWeakness, wobbliness, collapse or seizures, often within 10 to 60 minutes. A true emergency.
Onions, garlic, leeks and chivesStuffing, gravy, sauces, pigs in blankets, leftover meat cooked with seasoningWeakness, pale gums, tiredness. Signs can take several days to appear.
Cooked bonesTurkey carcass, ham bone, chicken wings, the kitchen binGagging, vomiting, straining, not eating. Bones splinter and can block or tear the gut.
Fatty foodTurkey skin, gravy, roast potatoes, sausage, cream and butterVomiting, a painful belly, hunched posture. Can trigger pancreatitis.
Macadamia nutsCookies, nut selections, cheese boardsWeakness in the back legs, wobbliness, trembling, high temperature
Alcohol and raw bread doughUnattended drinks, eggnog, rum sauce, rising dough for homemade breadWobbliness, vomiting, a swollen belly (dough keeps rising inside the stomach)

Decorations and the tree

These are the hazards most holiday guides miss.

  • Salt dough ornaments. Homemade salt dough decorations are full of salt and can cause salt poisoning if eaten. Hang them high or skip them.
  • Tinsel and ribbon. Long strings can bunch up the intestines as they pass through. Never pull a string you can see hanging from your dog's mouth or bottom; call your vet.
  • Glass baubles. They shatter into sharp pieces. Use shatterproof baubles on the lowest third of the tree.
  • Light cables. Chewed cables can cause burns to the mouth or electric shock. Run cables behind furniture or through a cable cover, and switch lights off when you go out.
  • Button batteries. Found in light-up decorations, cards, remotes and new toys. Swallowed batteries can burn through tissue within hours.
  • Snow globes. Some, especially imported ones, contain antifreeze (ethylene glycol), which is highly toxic. Keep them high up and clear up any breakage straight away.
  • Candles and potpourri. Wagging tails knock over candles, and potpourri can contain toxic essential oils and dried plants.
  • Edible decorations. Chocolate tree ornaments and candy canes. The wrappers are dangerous too.

Puppy-proof the tree in 5 steps: anchor it to the wall or a heavy base, put a baby gate or pen around it for young puppies, use shatterproof baubles on the bottom branches, cover the tree water, and skip tinsel completely.

Room-by-room holiday safety checklist

Walk through your home once with this list. It takes about 15 minutes.

Living room

  • Tree anchored and gated, shatterproof baubles on the lowest branches
  • No tinsel; ribbon and wrapping cleared after opening presents
  • Light cables covered or hidden behind furniture
  • No edible presents under the tree
  • Candles above tail height, or switched to LED candles

Kitchen

  • Bin has a locking lid, or is behind a closed door
  • Turkey carcass and bones double-bagged and taken outside straight away
  • Nothing cooling on low counters or the table edge
  • Rising bread dough out of reach
  • Dog kept out of the kitchen while you cook (a baby gate works well)

Dining room

  • Guests asked not to feed scraps
  • Drinks kept off low tables
  • Cheese board, nuts and mince pies cleared away after eating

Hallway and entrance

  • Guests' coats and bags hung up or put in a closed room (gum, medicines, chocolate)
  • Baby gate or closed door between your dog and the front door
  • Collar with ID tag on, and microchip details up to date

Your dog's quiet room

  • Bed or crate in a calm room away from the party
  • Water bowl, a chew or lick mat, and a familiar blanket
  • A "Please keep closed, dog resting" note on the door

Visitors and parties

  1. Set house rules and share them. No feeding the dog, keep doors and gates shut, and leave the dog alone when they go to their bed.
  2. Manage the front door. Put your dog behind a gate or in their quiet room when guests arrive, then bring them out once everyone has settled.
  3. Give them a job. A lick mat or snuffle game gives your dog something calm to do while the excitement passes.
  4. Watch children and dogs together. Lots of new children, noise and hugs can overwhelm even a friendly dog. Supervise, and let your dog walk away whenever they want.
  5. Know when your dog has had enough. Yawning, lip licking, turning away, hiding or growling all mean "I need a break". Take them to their quiet room.

Fireworks and New Year's Eve: a step-by-step plan

Many dogs find fireworks frightening. Frightened dogs can bolt through doors and even jump fences, so more dogs go missing around New Year's Eve and other fireworks nights than at most other times of year.

One to two weeks before

  • Set up a den: a crate or quiet corner covered with a blanket, in the most central, quietest room of the house.
  • Feed some meals and give chews in the den so your dog likes being there.
  • Check the ID tag, microchip details and garden fences.
  • If your dog has been very frightened before, speak to your vet now. There are calming products and medicines that need time to arrange.

On the day

  • Walk your dog in daylight, well before fireworks start, and keep them on a lead.
  • Feed dinner an hour or so before dark.
  • Close curtains, shut windows and doors, and turn on the TV or music to soften the bangs.
  • Take your dog out to the toilet on a lead, even in your own garden.
  • Stay home if you can, and stay calm.

During the fireworks

  • Let your dog choose where to hide. Don't pull them out from under furniture.
  • It's fine to comfort your dog if they come to you. Comforting a scared dog doesn't make the fear worse.
  • Offer a lick mat or chew if they're calm enough to take it.
  • Never punish fearful behaviour like barking, shaking or accidents.

The next morning

  • Check the garden for firework debris before letting your dog out.
  • Keep them on a lead for walks, as some people set off leftovers the next night.

Travelling with your dog over the holidays

Staying with family or heading away? Pack:

  • Their usual food (switching food suddenly can upset their stomach)
  • Bed or crate, plus a blanket that smells of home
  • Lead, collar with ID tag, and poo bags
  • Any medication and your vet's phone number
  • The number of an emergency vet near where you're staying
  • Chews or a lick mat for settling in a new place

When you arrive, walk round the house with your host and spot hazards together: their bin, low tables, other pets and any garden gaps.

What to do if your dog eats something dangerous

Act fast. Many poisonings are much easier to treat in the first hour or two.

  1. Remove your dog from whatever they've eaten and stop them eating more.
  2. Work out what and how much. Keep the packaging or wrapper, and note the time.
  3. Call your vet, or an out-of-hours emergency vet if your clinic is closed. Do this even if your dog seems completely fine. Many poisons take hours to show signs.
  4. Don't make your dog sick unless a vet tells you to. Home remedies like salt water can be dangerous.
  5. Take the packaging with you if you're asked to come in.

Pet poison helplines can also advise. A consultation fee may apply:

  • US: ASPCA Animal Poison Control (888) 426-4435, or Pet Poison Helpline (855) 764-7661
  • UK: Animal PoisonLine 01202 509000

Do this now: Save your vet's number and your nearest 24-hour emergency vet in your phone, and check the emergency vet's holiday opening hours.

5 common holiday mistakes

  1. Wrapping chocolate and putting it under the tree. Dogs smell it through the paper.
  2. Leaving the turkey carcass in the kitchen bin. Take it straight outside to a lidded bin.
  3. Letting guests "just give one little bit". Ten guests each giving one bit adds up to a very sick dog.
  4. Skipping the routine. Late walks, missed naps and unusual meal times make dogs more stressed and more likely to scavenge.
  5. Waiting to see if they're OK. With raisins, xylitol and chocolate, waiting for signs can mean waiting too long. Call your vet first.

Frequently asked questions

Are poinsettias poisonous to dogs?

Only mildly. They can cause drooling, mouth irritation and mild vomiting, but serious poisoning is rare. Holly and mistletoe are more of a concern. Keep all three out of reach.

How much chocolate is dangerous for a dog?

It depends on the type of chocolate and your dog's size. Dark and baking chocolate are far more dangerous than milk chocolate, and small dogs and puppies are at higher risk. Because the safe amount is so small for some dogs, call your vet whenever your dog eats any chocolate, and have the wrapper ready.

Is Christmas tree water safe for dogs?

No. It can contain bacteria, mould and preservatives or fertiliser that upset the stomach. Cover the stand with a tree skirt or a cut-down lid so your dog can't drink from it.

How can I keep my dog calm during fireworks?

Walk them before dark, set up a covered den in a quiet room, close curtains, play the TV or music, and stay with them. Comforting them is fine. If your dog panics every year, talk to your vet a few weeks beforehand about calming options.

Can dogs eat turkey?

A small amount of plain cooked turkey breast with no skin, bones, gravy or seasoning is fine. Skin, fatty bits and anything cooked with onion or garlic can make dogs ill, and cooked bones are dangerous.
