Your puppy has had a walk, a play and a meal, and they're still bouncing off the walls. The missing piece is usually mental exercise. A few minutes of brain games can tire a puppy out faster than another lap of the garden, and it builds focus and confidence at the same time.

Why brain games matter for puppies

Puppies are learning machines. Everything they sniff, chew and figure out is building the adult dog they'll become. When that curious brain has nothing to do, it finds its own projects: your shoes, the sofa cushions, your ankles.

Brain games give that energy somewhere useful to go. They:

  • Tire your puppy out without over-exercising growing joints
  • Build focus, which makes everyday training easier
  • Grow confidence as your puppy learns that problems can be solved
  • Reduce boredom behaviour like chewing, nipping and barking for attention
  • Strengthen your bond, because you become the source of all the fun

Before you start: 5 simple rules

  1. Keep sessions short. Three to five minutes is plenty for a young puppy. Stop while they still want more.
  2. Use tiny treats. Pea-sized pieces of soft treat, or part of their daily food allowance so you don't overfeed.
  3. Make it easy at first. Your puppy should win almost every time at the start. Success keeps them keen.
  4. Supervise every game. Puppies chew and swallow things they shouldn't. Put puzzle items away when the game ends.
  5. Pick a calm moment. Not straight after a meal, and not when your puppy is overtired and nippy.

10 brain games for puppies

1. Find It

EasyFrom 8 weeksTeaches: sniffing, focus

The perfect first brain game. Sniffing is hard mental work for dogs, and this game puts that nose to use.

How to play

  1. Show your puppy a treat, say "find it" and toss it a short distance on the floor.
  2. Praise as they gobble it up. Repeat five or six times.
  3. Once they rush off when you say "find it", place a treat just out of sight, behind a chair leg, and send them to find it.

Make it harder

Hide several treats around one room while your puppy waits in another, then let them search. Later, hide them in two rooms.

2. Which Hand?

EasyFrom 8 weeksTeaches: scent work, patience

How to play

  1. Let your puppy watch you put a treat in one hand. Close both hands and hold them out.
  2. Wait for your puppy to sniff or nudge the correct hand, then open it and let them have the treat.
  3. If they pick the empty hand, simply show it's empty and try again. No fuss.

Make it harder

Hide the treat behind your back so they can't see which hand it goes in. Now they have to rely on their nose.

Tip: If your puppy paws or nips at your hands, keep your fists closed and only open the hand when they nose it gently. They'll quickly learn that calm wins.

3. The Cup Game

MediumFrom 10 weeksTeaches: problem-solving

How to play

  1. Place one plastic cup upside down over a treat while your puppy watches.
  2. When they nose or paw the cup, lift it and let them eat the treat.
  3. Add a second empty cup. Let them choose, and reward the right pick.

Make it harder

Use three cups and slowly slide them around before your puppy chooses.

Safety: Use sturdy plastic cups, not paper ones that can be chewed and swallowed.

4. Muffin Tin Puzzle

EasyFrom 10 weeksTeaches: problem-solving, persistence

A homemade version of a dog puzzle toy that costs nothing.

How to play

  1. Drop a treat into a few cups of a muffin tin and let your puppy eat them out.
  2. Next time, cover one or two of the cups with tennis balls or soft toys.
  3. Your puppy has to work out how to move the balls to reach the treats.

Make it harder

Cover every cup, but only put treats in some of them so your puppy has to search.

Safety: Use balls too big to swallow, and put them away after the game. Never leave a puppy alone with a tennis ball they can chew apart.

5. Snuffle Towel

EasyFrom 8 weeksTeaches: sniffing, calm focus

How to play

  1. Lay an old towel flat and scatter some treats or kibble over it.
  2. Loosely roll the towel up from one end, trapping the treats inside.
  3. Put it down and let your puppy nose and paw it open to find the food.

Make it harder

Roll it tighter, or tie it in a loose knot once your puppy is a pro.

Tip: This is a great way to serve part of a meal on rainy days. Sniffing has a calming effect, so it's ideal before a nap.

6. Touch (Hand Target)

EasyFrom 8 weeksTeaches: focus, a useful cue

Half game, half training. Once learned, "touch" lets you move your puppy anywhere without pulling on their collar.

How to play

  1. Hold your flat palm a few centimetres from your puppy's nose.
  2. Most puppies will sniff it. The instant their nose touches your palm, say "yes!" and give a treat from your other hand.
  3. Repeat until they bump your hand every time, then add the word "touch".

Make it harder

Move your hand higher, lower, to the side, then a step away so your puppy has to come to you.

7. Hide and Seek

MediumFrom 10 weeksTeaches: recall, confidence

The best recall practice there is, disguised as fun.

How to play

  1. Ask a family member to gently hold your puppy, or wait until they're distracted.
  2. Step just out of sight, behind a door or around a corner, and call your puppy's name happily.
  3. When they find you, throw a party: praise, treats and play.

Make it harder

Hide in trickier places, like behind the shower curtain or in another room, and practise in the garden.

8. Name That Toy

MediumFrom 12 weeksTeaches: word learning

How to play

  1. Pick one favourite toy and give it a name, like "ball" or "duck".
  2. Say the name every time you play with it: "Get your duck!"
  3. After a few days, put it on the floor, say its name and reward your puppy for picking it up or touching it.

Make it harder

Put a second, different toy beside it and ask for the named one. Reward only the right choice. Add more toys over the coming weeks.

9. Cardboard Box Dig

EasyFrom 10 weeksTeaches: problem-solving, natural digging

How to play

  1. Fill a shallow cardboard box with scrunched-up paper and an old toy or two.
  2. Drop a handful of treats in among the paper.
  3. Let your puppy dig, rummage and rip their way to the treats.

Make it harder

Put a smaller box inside the big box, with treats in both.

Safety: Supervise closely. Shredding is fine, swallowing isn't. Remove any tape, staples and glossy printed paper first, and tidy up after the game.

10. Lick Mat Calm-Down

EasyFrom 8 weeksTeaches: settling, self-soothing

Not every brain game needs to be exciting. Licking is naturally soothing for dogs, which makes this the perfect wind-down before bed or when visitors arrive.

How to play

  1. Spread a thin layer of something tasty over a lick mat: wet puppy food, plain yoghurt or mashed banana.
  2. Give it to your puppy in their bed or crate and let them lick in peace.
  3. For longer sessions, freeze the mat first.

Safety: If you use peanut butter, check the label carefully. Some brands contain xylitol (sometimes listed as birch sugar), which is toxic to dogs.

Signs it's time to stop

Brain games are tiring, and an overtired puppy gets frustrated quickly. End the session and let them rest if you see:

  • Nipping, barking or pawing at you
  • Yawning, lip licking or looking away
  • Wandering off or losing interest
  • Getting the easy version wrong when they've done it before

Always try to finish on a win. If your puppy is struggling, go back to an easier version, reward the success and stop there.

Frequently asked questions

At what age can puppies start brain games?

Straight away. Simple games like Find It, Which Hand? and the Snuffle Towel are suitable from 8 weeks, the age most puppies come home. Keep sessions to a few minutes and make them easy to win.

How long should a brain game session be?

For young puppies, three to five minutes is enough. Two or three short sessions spread across the day work better than one long one.

Can brain games replace walks?

No. Puppies need both. Brain games are a great addition, especially on rainy days, before your puppy can go on walks in public places, or when you need them calm. They don't replace exploring the outside world.

My puppy gets frustrated and gives up. What should I do?

Make the game easier. Go back a step, give lots of easy wins, and only add difficulty slowly. Frustration is a sign the jump in difficulty was too big, not that your puppy can't do it.
