Quick answer

The best Christmas gifts for a puppy are things they'll use every day: a freezable rubber teething toy, a snuffle mat, a lick mat, a well-fitting adjustable harness and an engraved ID tag. Choose toys bigger than your puppy's mouth, check they pass the thumbnail test (below), and skip rawhide, cooked bones and anything with small parts.

It's your puppy's first Christmas, and the pet aisle is full of glittery stockings, squeaky reindeer and festive treats. Some of those make brilliant gifts. Others are choking hazards wrapped in a bow.

This guide picks out the gifts puppies actually need and use: chosen by age, by budget and by the problem you're dealing with right now, from teething to crying at night. For every gift you'll find exactly what to look for and what to avoid, plus simple safety tests you can do in the shop.

5 safety tests before you buy any toy

Do these in the shop or as soon as the parcel arrives. If a toy fails one, don't give it.

  1. The size test. The toy should be too big to fit fully inside your puppy's mouth, now and as they grow. Balls in particular should be wider than their open jaw.
  2. The thumbnail test. Press your thumbnail into a chew. It should dent slightly. If it doesn't give at all, it's hard enough to crack a puppy's tooth.
  3. The small parts test. Pull on eyes, buttons, tags and squeakers. Anything that comes loose can be swallowed.
  4. The label test. Look for "suitable for puppies" or a minimum age. Adult "power chewer" toys are often too hard for baby teeth.
  5. The wash test. Check it can go in the dishwasher or washing machine. Puppy toys get slobbery and dirty fast.

12 Christmas gifts puppies actually need

1. Freezable rubber teething toy

Best for teething3 to 6 monthsBudget: $10–20 / £8–15

Between about 3 and 6 months old, puppies lose their baby teeth and their gums are sore. A rubber toy you can freeze gives cold relief and a safe target for all that chewing.

What to look for

  • Natural rubber in a puppy-specific (softer) formula
  • A hollow centre you can stuff with wet food and freeze
  • Dishwasher safe

Avoid

Hard nylon "indestructible" chews made for adult dogs. They fail the thumbnail test for puppies.

Make it last longer: Fill it with wet puppy food, plug the end with a piece of kibble and freeze overnight. It turns a 2-minute toy into a 20-minute one.

2. Snuffle mat

Best for bored puppiesFrom 8 weeksBudget: $15–30 / £12–25

A fabric mat with fleece strips you hide kibble in. Your puppy has to sniff it out, which is surprisingly tiring. It's the shop-bought version of the snuffle towel in our brain games for puppies guide.

What to look for

  • Tightly stitched strips that don't pull out easily
  • A non-slip rubber base
  • Machine washable

Avoid

Leaving it out after the game. Puppies shred and swallow the strips when no one is watching.

3. Beginner puzzle feeder

Best for brainy puppiesFrom 10 weeksBudget: $12–25 / £10–20

A puzzle toy where your puppy slides, lifts or spins pieces to find treats.

What to look for

  • Marked as "Level 1" or "beginner". Most brands grade puzzles by difficulty.
  • No small removable pieces (bones or pegs that can be pulled out and swallowed)
  • A wide, heavy base so it doesn't flip

Avoid

Starting with a Level 2 or 3 puzzle. A frustrated puppy gives up and loses interest. Move up a level once they solve it in under a minute.

4. Lick mat

Best for calmingFrom 8 weeksBudget: $8–15 / £6–12

A textured silicone mat you spread with soft food. Licking is naturally soothing, which makes it perfect for Christmas Day, when the house is loud and full of visitors.

What to look for

  • Food-grade silicone
  • Suction cups or a heavy base
  • Freezer and dishwasher safe

Avoid

Leaving it with your puppy once it's licked clean. It quickly becomes a chew toy.

5. Heartbeat comfort toy

Best for night crying8 to 16 weeksBudget: $20–35 / £15–30

A soft toy with a battery-powered "heartbeat" inside, and often a warming pack. It mimics the feeling of sleeping against mum and littermates, which can help a new puppy settle in the crate.

What to look for

  • A heartbeat unit that sits in a fully closed pocket with a secure zip or Velcro
  • A washable outer cover
  • If it has a heat pack, one designed for pets

Avoid

Leaving it with a puppy who's started to chew it. Remove it at the first sign of ripping; batteries are dangerous if swallowed.

6. Washable crate pad

Best for crate trainingAll agesBudget: $20–40 / £15–35

A comfy crate makes crate training easier. Puppies have accidents, so washability matters more than looks.

What to look for

  • Sized to your crate, with no big gap at the edges
  • A removable, machine-washable cover (buy two so one is always clean)
  • Flat or low sides for young puppies

Avoid

Big fluffy beds with stuffing for a chewer. If your puppy starts shredding bedding, switch to a vet bed or a folded blanket until the chewing phase passes.

7. Adjustable harness

Best for walksFrom 12 weeksBudget: $15–35 / £12–30

A harness spreads pressure across the chest instead of the neck, and a good one is much harder for a wriggly puppy to back out of.

What to look for

  • A Y-shaped front that sits between the front legs, not across the shoulders
  • At least 4 adjustment points, so it grows with your puppy
  • A good fit: you can slide two fingers under every strap, and no more

Avoid

Buying a size up "to grow into". A loose harness is easy to slip out of. Adjust it as they grow and size up when you run out of adjustment.

8. Engraved ID tag

Most important giftAll agesBudget: $8–20 / £6–15

Christmas means open doors, visitors and fireworks on New Year's Eve, which are prime conditions for an escape. An ID tag is the fastest way a stranger can get your puppy home.

What to put on it

  • Your surname and phone number
  • Your address or at least your postcode / ZIP code
  • "I'm microchipped" if there's space

Good to know: In the UK, dogs in public must wear a collar with a tag showing the owner's name and address, and microchipping is compulsory. Rules vary elsewhere, so check your local requirements. Keep your microchip details up to date too.

9. Treat pouch

Best for trainingFrom 8 weeksBudget: $10–20 / £8–15

Rewarding within a second or two of good behaviour is what makes training work. A pouch on your hip means treats are always within reach.

What to look for

  • A magnetic or hinged opening you can work with one hand
  • A clip or belt so it sits on your hip
  • A washable lining

10. Grooming starter kit

Best for handlingFrom 8 weeksBudget: $15–30 / £12–25

Puppies who get used to brushing, nail trims and having their paws touched early are far easier (and less stressed) at the groomer and the vet.

What to include

  • A soft bristle brush or rubber grooming mitt
  • Small puppy nail clippers with a safety guard
  • Gentle puppy shampoo

How to use it: Touch a paw, give a treat. Brush one stroke, give a treat. Keep sessions to 1 to 2 minutes and stop before your puppy wriggles.

11. Dog advent calendar

Most festiveFrom 12 weeksBudget: $8–20 / £5–15

A fun daily ritual for December, as long as you choose carefully.

What to look for

  • Treats labelled as suitable for puppies
  • Small treats, because they count towards your puppy's food for the day
  • A short, simple ingredient list if your puppy has a sensitive stomach

Avoid

Hanging it where your puppy can reach it. Dogs can smell every treat through the cardboard and will happily eat all 24 at once.

Christmas Day tips for puppy owners

  1. Turn present opening into a game. Wrap one toy loosely in plain brown paper with no tape or ribbon and let your puppy rip it open. It's the Cardboard Box Dig from our brain games guide, Christmas edition.
  2. Clear up wrapping straight away. Ribbon, tinsel and tape cause dangerous gut blockages if swallowed.
  3. Don't put edible gifts under the tree. Your puppy will find the chocolate before you do.
  4. Give your puppy a quiet room. Set up their crate or bed in a calm space with a lick mat so they can escape the noise.
  5. Stick to the routine. Keep meals, toilet breaks and naps at their usual times. An overtired puppy on Christmas Day is a bitey puppy.
  6. Brief your visitors. Ask guests not to feed the puppy from the table and to keep doors and gates closed.

4 common gift-buying mistakes

  1. Buying for the photo, not the puppy. A cute reindeer outfit is worn once. A teething toy is used every day.
  2. Giving every toy at once. Put most toys away and rotate 3 or 4 at a time. Swapping them every few days keeps each one exciting.
  3. Choosing adult-strength chews. Always check the label says it's suitable for puppies.
  4. Forgetting the boring essentials. An ID tag, enzyme cleaner for accidents and a spare crate cover make life easier than another squeaky toy.

Frequently asked questions

What should I get my puppy for their first Christmas?

For a puppy under 12 weeks, a heartbeat comfort toy, a lick mat and an engraved ID tag. For a puppy aged 3 to 6 months, a freezable teething toy, a snuffle mat and an adjustable harness. See the age table above.

Are stuffed toys safe for puppies?

Only with supervision. Choose toys with embroidered eyes and no small squeakers, and take the toy away as soon as your puppy starts ripping seams. Swallowed stuffing can cause blockages.

Can my puppy have Christmas dinner?

A small piece of plain cooked turkey breast with no skin, bones, gravy or seasoning is fine as a treat. Keep stuffing, gravy, pigs in blankets, Christmas pudding, mince pies and chocolate well out of reach, as these contain fat, onions, garlic, raisins or chocolate that can make dogs ill.

How many toys does a puppy need?

About 5 to 8 is plenty: two or three chew toys, one or two puzzle or sniffing toys, a comfort toy and a tug or fetch toy. Rotate them rather than leaving them all out.

Should I give someone a puppy as a Christmas present?

Not as a surprise. A dog can live 10 to 15 years or more, and the new owner should choose their puppy and be ready for the time, cost and commitment. A better gift is something to help them prepare, like our free survival guide, a training programme or a voucher towards their puppy's first essentials. Christmas is also a hectic time to settle a new puppy, so many reputable breeders and rescues recommend waiting until the holidays are over.
